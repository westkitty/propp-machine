# The Tale of Marfa and Count Moth

Nobody in a town whose church bell rang a minute early, out of nerves expected much of Marfa Sootfoot, who could whistle in three directions at once; the neighbours even less.

Then Count Moth came and took Princess Vasilka the Unhurried away.

The news went round, and at last it reached Marfa.

“I will go,” said Marfa.

So Marfa left home. Where three ways met, Marfa took the road that went through the forest, where the trees whispered half of everything.

At a crossroads, the girl met a ferryman with no shadow at a river that had forgotten its other bank. “Do me a service,” it said, and its eyes were very old.

Our Marfa solved the puzzle quickly.

A ferryman with no shadow opened a sack that held three things: a jar of borrowed thunder; a ball of red thread that knows the way; a birch-bark flute that makes the dead dance politely. A ferryman with no shadow gave Marfa a birch-bark flute that makes the dead dance politely.

Wouldn’t you know it, Marfa was led through the forest, where the trees whispered half of everything as far as the Birch Thicket of Unfinished Sentences.

Princess Vasilka the Unhurried was free.

Then Marfa turned back toward a town whose church bell rang a minute early, out of nerves.

The Count flew in a mortar after the girl, shrieking.

Marfa played the flute, and everything with bones began to dance, and escaped.

The elder brothers Glib and Gleb said that they had done everything.

There was one more task: to find which of twelve identical doves was the princess.

With the cat’s help, our Marfa did it before the cock crowed.

Then Princess Vasilka the Unhurried recognised Marfa.

The elder brothers Glib and Gleb tried to slip out by the scullery, but the goose remembered.

And the Count was made to count the crows of the kingdom, and is counting still.

Marfa and Princess Vasilka the Unhurried were wed, and lived long.
