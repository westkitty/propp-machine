# The Tale of Agafya and the Tallow King

Once, in a hut beside a river that ran uphill on Sundays, lived Agafya Last-of-Eleven, whom nobody remembered to name until she was six.

Then the village elder with the brass ear-trumpet went off to sell a cow and a silence, and Agafya was left in charge.

The village elder with the brass ear-trumpet pinned a note to the door: do not open the green door at the end of the passage. Agafya said “Of course”, and began to wonder at once.

By the third day, Agafya Last-of-Eleven broke the rule, as rules are broken.

At dusk, the Tallow King asked the wind where the precious things were kept.

A chattering neighbour told the monarch everything.

Then the waxen king came and stole a bride who could laugh, and the whole land went dim.

The village elder with the brass ear-trumpet rang the cracked bell, and it was the girl who answered.

Agafya decided to set things right.

At dawn, Agafya took the road. Past the post with the rude carving, Agafya chose the road down a ladder of roots, deeper than the wells go.

On the way Agafya met an old woman knitting fog on a stump at the edge of the road, who said: “First, help me.”

“Ask me a harder one,” said our Agafya, and answered.

An old woman knitting fog unfolded a rag that held three things: a jar of borrowed thunder; a horse-hair that summons a horse of storm; a bone comb that grows forests when thrown. Three days later an old woman knitting fog pulled from a sleeve a bone comb that grows forests when thrown. “Use it once.”

So Agafya came to the Copper Kingdom under the earth, led down a ladder of roots, deeper than the wells go.

At last, the girl stood before the waxen king, a monarch who melted a little whenever he lied. Wouldn’t you know it, the waxen king proposed a contest.

And so the monarch was defeated.

Then, Agafya found a bride who could laugh again.

Then Agafya turned back toward a hut beside a river that ran uphill on Sundays.

The waxen king scuttled on a hundred borrowed legs after Agafya, shrieking.

Three days later a little wooden doll that ate porridge carried the girl away to safety.

Within sight of the roofs of home, a general with polished boots and no battles pushed Agafya down a well and went home with the credit.

Up out of the dark, Agafya came back into the light, cross but whole.

“It was us,” said a general with polished boots and no battles, and the Tsar clapped, slowly.

There was one more task: to answer a riddle that had no right answer.

Before the candle was half gone the task was done, neatly.

Now everyone recognised Agafya at last.

The lie fell over by itself: a general with polished boots and no battles had done nothing at all.

Then there was a wedding.
