# The Tale of Agafya and Bonelace the Deathless

Nobody in a hut beside a river that ran uphill on Sundays expected much of Agafya Last-of-Eleven, whom nobody remembered to name until she was six; the cat least of all.

One day the hero’s grandmother went away to market and did not come back for a long time.

Under the window, Bonelace the Deathless counted the family twice.

Then, a chattering neighbour told Bonelace everything.

“Lacking a feather of the Ember-Bird,” sighed the Tsaritsa of Nine Rivers, “we may as well eat the furniture.”

The girl tightened her belt and resolved to go.

The girl set out along the road. Where three ways met, Agafya took the road through the forest, where the trees whispered half of everything.

There sat a ferryman with no shadow at a river that had forgotten its other bank, who asked our Agafya a question.

Being kind, the girl bowed low.

A ferryman with no shadow unfolded a rag that held three things: a bone comb that grows forests when thrown; boots that walk only forward; a birch-bark flute that makes the dead dance politely. Out of a knot in the wood, a ferryman with no shadow brought a bone comb that grows forests when thrown, and somewhere a bell rang.

Our Agafya was led through the forest, where the trees whispered half of everything as far as the Birch Thicket of Unfinished Sentences.

By the cat’s help, the girl won back a feather of the Ember-Bird.

With the prize in her cap, our Agafya took the road back to a hut beside a river that ran uphill on Sundays.

Bonelace flew in a mortar after the girl, shrieking.

Just in time, the cat hid her in a cuckoo’s egg.

Within sight of the roofs of home, the elder brothers Glib and Gleb sold Agafya to a passing bear and went home with the credit.

A stray thread of the road led the girl on.

With help from the cat, Agafya climbed free, thinner but alive.

The girl came home a second time.

The elder brothers Glib and Gleb sent dogs after Agafya.

The girl hid in a haystack until the dogs grew bored.

Agafya came back in rags, and nobody knew her.

“It was us,” said the elder brothers Glib and Gleb, and the court believed it.

The court demanded proof: to answer a riddle that had no right answer.

Before the candle was half gone the task was done, neatly.

Tsarevich Lev Pale-Hands looked twice and cried, “This is the one!”

The lie fell over by itself: the elder brothers Glib and Gleb had done nothing at all.

And Bonelace was made to count the crows of the kingdom, and is counting still.

Then there was a wedding.
