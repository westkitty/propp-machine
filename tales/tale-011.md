# The Tale of Savva and Count Moth

In a village where the rooster crowed backwards there lived Savva the Turnip-Brave, who once outstared a turnip.

“Without the singing apple that told the truth,” sighed the Tsaritsa of Nine Rivers, “a kingdom is just a field with a fence.”

The news went round, and at last it reached our Savva.

“Give me a crust and a blessing, and I will go,” said our Savva.

And Savva set off, it may be near, it may be far; the tale is quick, the road is not. Where three ways met, our Savva took the road that went through the forest, where the trees whispered half of everything.

The Grandmother of Owls in a hut built entirely of locked doors asked for a name and a kindness: “First a task, then a road.”

“That one is easy,” said the lad, and the answer was correct, which surprised everyone.

The Grandmother of Owls unfolded a rag that held three things: boots that walk only forward; a handkerchief that unfolds into a lake; a birch-bark flute that makes the dead dance politely. From under the moss, the Grandmother of Owls brought a birch-bark flute that makes the dead dance politely, and somewhere a bell rang.

At last, Savva stood before Count Moth. The Count set out the scales of the dead, and they played three rounds.

And so the Count was defeated.

By the gift’s doing, Savva won back the singing apple that told the truth.

Savva went home.

The Count scuttled on a hundred borrowed legs after our Savva, singing a lullaby, which was worse.

In time a cat with nine lives, six already spent carried our Savva away to safety.

The lad came back as a cook’s boy, and the dog alone wagged.

There was one more task: to eat a whole oven of bread before dawn.

The task was done by morning.

Then Tsarevich Lev Pale-Hands recognised Savva.

Our Savva stepped through the cat’s ear and out the other and came out handsome.

The Count was put in a barrel and rolled into the sea, which rolled him back out, ashamed.

Our Savva married Tsarevich Lev Pale-Hands, and there was a feast.
